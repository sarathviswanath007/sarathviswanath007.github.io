How I work

Quality isn't a phase at the end of a sprint. It's a discipline that either accelerates delivery or blocks it, and I've spent over a decade making sure it's the former.

My approach: catch risk before it reaches production, automate the repetitive so engineers focus on the hard problems, and treat AI not as a shortcut but as a force multiplier for how teams test, and increasingly, for what teams are testing.

I've led that shift across agritech, insurance, automotive, and e-commerce platforms, each with its own definition of "quality that matters."

The next chapter of this work isn't optimizing how AI helps us test. It's defining how we test AI itself.

AI + Quality Engineering

For decades, a test had one job: compare actual output to an expected output. Pass or fail. Generative AI breaks that model. The "correct" answer isn't fixed, it's a distribution.

A recommendation engine, a chatbot, a GenAI-powered feature can be technically functional and still wrong: hallucinated, inconsistent across runs, subtly biased, or unsafe.

Quality Engineering hasn't caught up to this. Most teams are still applying deterministic thinking, pass/fail asserts, to systems that are fundamentally probabilistic. I'm actively building my expertise in this space: what does an evaluation framework look like when correctness is a spectrum? How do you regression-test a prompt? How do you validate relevance, consistency, and safety at scale?

This is where I'm placing my next 12 years.

Case studies

Three problems, solved end to end.

Shift-left quality

Moving defects out of production and into the pull request

Challenge

Quality checks lived at the end of the SDLC. Defects surfaced late, often in production, where they were slow and expensive to fix and eroded release confidence.

Action

Embedded quality early: contributed test thinking into design and refinement, moved unit, contract, and API checks into CI, and made automated validation a gate on every pull request.

Innovation

Reframed quality as a design-time concern, not a release-time one. Risk was assessed before a line of code was written, so the riskiest changes carried the most coverage from day one.

Impact

Defects were caught in review and CI instead of production. Feedback reached engineers in minutes, not sprints, and release confidence became the default rather than the exception.

Regression automation

From an 8-hour bottleneck to a 25-minute pipeline

Challenge

A cloud-native, microservices platform relied on manual regression before every release. A full cycle took a working day, so QA became the bottleneck between "feature done" and "feature shipped."

Action

Led the design and rollout of an automated regression suite across UI, API, and integration layers, replacing manual execution with repeatable, CI-integrated coverage.

Innovation

Restructured the suite around risk-based prioritization, so high-impact paths ran first and fast feedback reached engineers before low-risk edge cases did.

Impact

Regression time dropped 19x. Release model shifted from feature-blocked to continuous: ship on Definition of Done, not on a fixed calendar.

AI adoption in Quality Engineering

Turning ad hoc AI use into a team-wide capability

Challenge

AI tooling was available, but adoption was inconsistent. Engineers used it ad hoc, with no shared method, leading to inefficient prompting and inconsistent output quality.

Action

Built and rolled out reusable prompting standards for the QE team, a shared practice rather than an individual habit, for AI-assisted test design, analysis, and debugging.

Innovation

Treated prompt engineering as a team capability to be systematized. Standardized patterns meant the team operated within existing token allocation more efficiently.

Impact

20-30% team-wide adoption of AI-assisted workflows, more consistent output quality, and less repetitive manual test-design work.

This is also the operational foundation for the next frontier, team-wide AI fluency at scale: validating AI systems themselves, not just using AI to test faster.

Leadership philosophy

Two principles I operate by.

Lead from behind. Stand in front when it breaks.

I give my team the space to own decisions and take the credit when things go right. When something breaks, I'm the one who steps forward, not to assign blame but to absorb it. Ownership isn't a title you hold when things are easy; it's what you do when they're not.

Failure is the cost of innovation, and I make it safe to pay.

I don't optimize for a team that never fails. I optimize for a team that tries. If people are only ever hitting safe, predictable outcomes, they've stopped experimenting, and a QE function that's stopped experimenting can't keep up with how fast quality itself is changing.
