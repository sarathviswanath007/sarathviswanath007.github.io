Quality Engineering Doesn't Disappear. It Moves Upstream.

Sarath Viswanath July 2026 9 min read

For the past few months I've been circling one question: how will AI change the future of Quality Engineering? The more I explored, the more I realized I was asking the wrong question.

Like most people in the profession, I started where the anxiety starts. Will AI replace testers? Will Quality Engineering disappear? Will testing become fully automated? Those questions feel urgent, but they're not the ones that matter. The real question sits one level up:

How does AI change the Software Development Life Cycle itself?

Once you look at software delivery through that lens, the future gets much clearer. This isn't a story about job titles surviving or dying. It's a story about where responsibility for quality moves, and who holds it.

Phase 1: AI accelerates Quality Engineering

Over the next two to five years, Quality Engineering stays highly relevant. In fact, demand grows for professionals who understand a new class of problems:

  • AI testing and AI evaluation frameworks
  • LLM validation and prompt evaluation
  • AI risk assessment and Responsible AI
  • AI-assisted test generation
  • AI governance

As organizations ship AI-powered products, these skills become more valuable, not less. For today's Quality Engineers, this is the natural next step, the right investment for the near term. But it isn't the final destination.

Phase 2: AI changes the economics of software engineering

Historically, development was the bottleneck. Engineers spent weeks building features. Quality teams then validated what had been built. That separation made sense precisely because building was the slow part of the process.

AI breaks that equation. When an engineer can generate production-ready code, documentation, unit tests, integration tests, and UI tests in minutes, development is no longer the slowest stage of delivery. So organizations start asking the obvious question:

Why hand work to another specialist when one engineer can own it end to end with AI?

This is where the traditional developer evolves into what I'd call an AI Product Engineer, someone who owns the complete lifecycle of a feature: understanding the requirement, designing the solution, building with AI, generating and reviewing tests, validating outputs, deploying, monitoring production, and improving continuously.

Testing doesn't disappear. It becomes part of engineering.

Human judgment doesn't disappear, it moves closer to the product

Organizations didn't invest in independent QA purely for test execution. They invested in independent human judgment. Someone had to ask the questions a script never will:

  • Does this actually solve the customer's problem?
  • Does the workflow make sense?
  • What happens in the unexpected cases?
  • Is this the experience we intended?

AI can't answer those questions. Humans still must. What changes is where that judgment sits, it moves closer to the people who understand the customer best. The Product Lead and the AI Product Engineer increasingly validate business outcomes together. The engineer ensures the software behaves correctly. The Product Lead ensures it delivers the intended value.

Quality Engineering shifts from performing every validation activity to helping teams make better quality decisions, coaching on user-centric thinking, risk-based testing, exploratory testing, edge-case analysis, and quality-first engineering practices.

One SDLC won't fit every organization

Not every company will adopt the same operating model. And the deciding factor isn't AI, it's the cost of failure.

Low cost of failure

Where defects cause only inconvenience or minor business impact, quality folds directly into AI Product Engineering teams. Handoffs shrink, ownership goes end to end.

High cost of failure

Where failure means financial loss, safety risk, regulatory penalties, or lost trust, independent Quality Engineering stays critical, tied directly to governance and risk.

Banking, financial services, aviation, healthcare, medical devices, automotive safety, and critical infrastructure will keep strong independent quality functions, because in those domains quality is inseparable from compliance, risk, and trust.

The principle is simple: the higher the cost of failure, the stronger the need for independent quality oversight. The lower the cost, the more quality responsibilities move into engineering teams.

So does Quality Engineering disappear?

No. I think it becomes more important. What changes is the nature of the work. The execution-heavy activities increasingly move to AI and AI Product Engineers:

  • Manual regression testing
  • Repetitive test-case creation
  • Basic automation scripting
  • Routine validation

Quality Engineering moves upstream, into work that shapes how quality is built rather than checked at the end:

  • Defining quality policies for AI-generated software
  • Establishing AI evaluation standards
  • Creating test strategies
  • Coaching engineers on quality engineering practices
  • Owning the outcomes of quality
  • Defining release governance
  • Measuring quality
  • Assessing technical and business risk
  • Partnering with security and compliance teams
The uncomfortable part

The importance of Quality Engineering isn't shrinking, but the number of traditional QE roles probably will. Not because quality matters less, but because AI lets every engineer own a far larger slice of the lifecycle.

What should Quality Engineers do to stay relevant?

Learning AI testing and AI evaluation frameworks is the right move for the next few years. It isn't enough for the long term. The stronger play is to gradually expand into:

  • Software engineering and AI-assisted development
  • Product thinking
  • Architecture and systems thinking
  • Understanding Risk management

Some Quality Engineers will become AI Product Engineers. Others will move into Engineering Excellence, AI Governance, or Engineering Leadership. The common thread is straightforward: the future belongs to professionals who combine engineering capability with quality thinking.

What's worth keeping

Every major technology shift has changed how software is built. Each one moved a different part of the equation. AI isn't removing the need for quality. It's redistributing responsibility for achieving it.

  • Cloud transformed infrastructure. Where software runs stopped being a constraint.
  • DevOps transformed delivery. How fast and how often we ship changed permanently.
  • AI is transforming ownership. Handoffs give way to end-to-end responsibility, supported by AI.

Quality stays essential, but the way organizations build it, and the roles responsible for it, will change dramatically.

The challenge isn't protecting yesterday's job titles. It's helping design tomorrow's engineering organizations, where quality isn't something we hand off, but something we engineer together.
